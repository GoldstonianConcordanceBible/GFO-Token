Global Social Experiment and Web4 Governance Repository Charter

Repository status: Canonical research, governance, and prompt-backend repository
Project type: Version-controlled research infrastructure and implementation framework
Primary domains: Web4, Web4 NIL, artificial intelligence governance, autonomous agents, decentralized governance, identity rights, education, theology, cultural interpretation, healthcare, repository licensing, and human-centered institutional design
Maintainer: Project governance team
Version: 0.1.0-draft
Canonical authority: This repository

⸻

1. Repository Purpose

This repository is the canonical backend for the Global Social Experiment and its related Web4 research, governance, education, licensing, and implementation programs.

It is designed to support two connected functions:

1. a transparent, version-controlled research and governance archive; and
2. a structured knowledge backend that allows website users to submit prompts and receive grounded responses based on approved repository content.

The repository must preserve the distinction between:

* research evidence;
* research questions;
* hypotheses;
* model-generated analysis;
* researcher interpretation;
* governance proposals;
* approved policies;
* technical requirements;
* legal claims;
* theological or mythological interpretation;
* commercial services;
* and operational implementations.

No single document, model response, website output, token, publication, or project announcement may override the canonical repository structure.

⸻

2. Primary Research Question

The primary research question governing the repository is:

How can a version-controlled, AI-assisted governance repository be used to design, test, document, and improve human-centered rules for identity, autonomous agents, tokenized communities, creator compensation, and institutional coordination in an emerging Web4 environment?

All repository content must either:

* contribute evidence relevant to this question;
* define a related research question;
* document a method used to investigate it;
* preserve a finding or disagreement;
* translate a finding into governance or technical requirements;
* or identify an implementation, limitation, risk, or future research need.

⸻

3. Repository Objectives

The repository exists to:

1. document the Global Social Experiment as a formal research program;
2. preserve the derivation and development of Web4 as an emergent research construct;
3. define and test the Web4 NIL framework;
4. document the modified multi-model Delphi methodology;
5. preserve model consensus, dissent, errors, and revisions;
6. distinguish enacted law from proposed legislation, guidance, interpretation, and project preference;
7. connect governance policies to schemas, workflows, code, tests, permissions, and audit evidence;
8. support a prompt-based website without allowing uncontrolled model output to become canonical knowledge;
9. create reusable and licensable governance artifacts;
10. support books, academic articles, conference presentations, educational materials, consulting engagements, and implementation projects;
11. preserve institutional memory through Git history, issues, pull requests, releases, and archived versions;
12. create a defensible record of how major claims were developed, challenged, accepted, revised, or rejected.

4. Definition of the Global Social Experiment

The Global Social Experiment is a connected research environment examining how humans, artificial intelligence systems, autonomous agents, decentralized communities, publishers, educational institutions, cultural organizations, healthcare systems, and governance repositories may coordinate in an emerging Web4 environment.

It is not limited to one:

* token;
* DAO;
* artificial intelligence model;

5. Definition of Web4

Web4 is treated in this repository as an emergent and testable research construct.

It must not be represented as an established universal standard unless supported by external adoption and independently verifiable evidence.

The working hypothesis is that Web4 describes environments in which some combination of the following interact:

* humans;
* autonomous or semi-autonomous artificial intelligence agents;
* decentralized or portable identity;
* synthetic personas;
* programmable authorization;
* machine-readable consent;
* machine payments;
* tokenized or digitally coordinated communities;
* version-controlled governance;
* policy-to-code traceability;
* human override and revocation rights;
* and institutional accountability.

The repository must preserve open questions regarding whether Web4 requires:

* blockchain;
* tokens;
* autonomous agents;
* decentralized identity;
* machine payments;
* smart contracts;
* or a specific technical architecture.

Web4 may ultimately be validated, revised, divided into subconstructs, merged with another framework, or rejected.

6. Definition of Web4 NIL

Web4 NIL is a proposed governance framework for the authorized use of human-linked identity elements in agentic, synthetic, tokenized, and digitally mediated environments.

Potential identity elements include:

* name;
* image;
* likeness;
* voice;
* biography;
* signature;
* writing style;
* personality representation;
* avatar;
* synthetic media;
* digital replica;
* digital twin;
* conversational persona;
* memory representation;
* reputation;
* credentials;
* data-derived identity;
* and autonomous agents acting under human-associated authority.

Authorization for one identity element must not automatically imply authorization for another.

Authorization must be capable of specifying:

* identity element;
* purpose;
* platform;
* jurisdiction;
* territory;
* duration;
* compensation;
* modification rights;
* sublicensing rights;
* model-training rights;
* archival rights;
* disclosure requirements;
* revocation conditions;
* suspension conditions;
* termination conditions;
* and surviving uses.

Web4 NIL is not represented as a substitute for legal counsel or jurisdiction-specific rights analysis.

7. The Unknown-Unknown Research Problem

The repository is designed in part to investigate unknown unknowns.

An unknown unknown is a risk, interaction, consequence, capability, or governance problem that has not yet been recognized clearly enough to become a formal question.

The repository distinguishes four categories:

7.1 Known Knowns

Matters currently understood and supported by sufficient evidence.

7.2 Known Unknowns

Questions that are recognized but remain unanswered.

7.3 Unknown Knowns

Assumptions, embedded practices, inherited rules, or tacit knowledge influencing the system without adequate examination.

7.4 Unknown Unknowns

Questions, risks, and consequences that have not yet become visible.

The repository cannot eliminate unknown unknowns.

It must instead create mechanisms for identifying and responding to them through:

* issue reporting;
* incident logging;
* model disagreement;
* human review;
* version control;
* red-team analysis;
* legal verification;
* technical testing;
* policy revision;
* and periodic research-question updates.

9. Prompt-Backend Rule

The website prompt system must use repository content as its governed knowledge backend.

The prompt system may:

* retrieve approved repository chunks;
* summarize canonical material;
* compare policies;
* explain defined concepts;
* identify unresolved research questions;
* generate implementation checklists from approved requirements;
* and provide citations to repository files.

The prompt system must not:

* silently rewrite canonical content;
* convert model speculation into repository fact;
* present proposed legislation as enacted law;
* imply legal certification;
* invent consent records;
* invent compensation terms;
* invent technical audits;
* fabricate partnerships or endorsements;
* expose restricted records;
* or treat theological, mythological, artistic, or marketing language as empirical evidence.

All responses must include provenance metadata sufficient to identify the source chunks used.

10. Content Authority Levels

Every knowledge chunk must carry one authority level.

Level 0 — Unreviewed Input

Raw notes, transcripts, prompts, comments, imported material, or preliminary model output.

Level 1 — Research Candidate

Material accepted for structured review but not approved as a finding or policy.

Level 2 — Reviewed Research Material

Material reviewed for relevance, provenance, and classification.

Level 3 — Approved Research Finding

A finding approved under the repository review process with documented evidence and limitations.

Level 4 — Approved Governance Policy

A policy adopted through the authorized governance process.

Level 5 — Technical Control

A verified schema, workflow, permission, test, or code implementation associated with an approved policy.

Level 6 — Externally Verified or Audited

Material independently reviewed by an appropriately qualified external party.

No language model may independently raise content to Levels 3 through 6.

12. Legal Accuracy Rule

Legal claims must distinguish among:

* enacted statutes;
* regulations;
* case law;
* proposed legislation;
* executive actions;
* agency guidance;
* government commentary;
* industry interpretation;
* academic interpretation;
* and project policy.

Proposed legislation must never be described as binding law.

Legal claims must include:

* jurisdiction;
* authority type;
* citation or source;
* effective date;
* verification date;
* verification status;
* reviewer;
* and next review date.

The repository and website must state that they do not provide legal, tax, accounting, investment, privacy, cybersecurity, or regulatory advice.

13. Delphi Method Rule

The modified LLM Delphi study must preserve:

* independent first-round responses;
* anonymized synthesis;
* second-round reconsideration;
* third-round stability review;
* model-specific dissent;
* unsupported claims;
* factual errors;
* methodological limitations;
* and final consensus classifications.

LLM consensus must be described as structured model convergence, not human expert consensus.

Models may serve as:

* structured reviewers;
* adversarial critics;
* comparative reasoning systems;
* hypothesis generators;
* architecture evaluators;
* and objects of research.

Models may not serve as final legal, ethical, regulatory, technical, or institutional authorities.

The following domains must remain separately labeled:

Governance

Policies, roles, authority, reserved matters, voting, appeals, emergency powers, and accountability.

Technical Implementation

Schemas, APIs, code, smart contracts, tests, permissions, credentials, deployments, and audit records.

Law and Regulation

Verified legal authority, proposed legislation, guidance, cases, and professional-review triggers.

Education

Curriculum, student activities, assessments, learning outcomes, consent, and research protections.

Theology and Mythology

Interpretive, doctrinal, narrative, symbolic, and pedagogical material.

Commercial Activity

Consulting, licensing, certification, implementation services, pricing, and customer delivery.

Marketing and Artistic Expression

Memes, promotional writing, token narratives, creative campaigns, branding, and community media.

Material may reference multiple domains, but each claim must retain its original classification.

15. Human Authority Principle

Human beings must retain meaningful authority over systems acting through their identity, rights, reputation, credentials, compensation, or legal interests.

Human authority must include, where applicable:

* informed authorization;
* purpose limitation;
* review rights;
* correction rights;
* suspension rights;
* revocation rights;
* termination rights;
* compensation transparency;
* access to records;
* dispute procedures;
* and emergency protection.

DAO votes, token ownership, model outputs, repository commits, or technical permissions must not automatically override legally or contractually protected human rights.

⸻

16. Documentation Readiness Versus Activation Readiness

The repository distinguishes documentation readiness from activation readiness.

Documentation Readiness

The required policies, schemas, agreements, procedures, roles, and records have been drafted and reviewed.

Activation Readiness

The system has completed the legal, technical, security, privacy, operational, and professional-review requirements necessary for live use.

A project may be documentation-ready without being activation-ready.

No readiness score may imply legal compliance, regulatory approval, investment safety, technical security, or professional certification unless independently established.

17. Repository Governance Principles

The repository must operate according to:

* version control;
* least privilege;
* separation of duties;
* documented review;
* branch protection;
* code ownership;
* provenance;
* reversible change where technically possible;
* dissent preservation;
* conflict disclosure;
* incident logging;
* dependency tracking;
* and release discipline.

Major policy changes must be proposed through pull requests.

Breaking changes must be identified in release notes.

Rejected proposals should remain discoverable unless removal is required for privacy, security, legal, or safety reasons.

18. Privacy Principle

Transparency does not require exposing private or sensitive information.

The public repository must not contain:

* private legal identities not intended for disclosure;
* signatures;
* tax records;
* private contracts;
* authentication credentials;
* API keys;
* private wallet mappings;
* biometric templates;
* confidential payment records;
* medical records;
* student records;
* protected minor information;
* or confidential client information.

Public records should contain references, hashes, status indicators, or redacted summaries where appropriate.

20. Non-Endorsement Principle

Reference to an institution, platform, school, museum, company, government body, model provider, blockchain, publication, or individual does not imply:

* partnership;
* sponsorship;
* endorsement;
* approval;
* research participation;
* certification;
* or institutional adoption.

Institutional participation must be documented through appropriate written authorization.

Publicly available material may be analyzed as public evidence when permitted, but it must not be represented as licensed data or partnership material without authorization.

21. Research Ethics Principle

Research involving people, students, minors, patients, visitors, clients, employees, or community participants may require:

* informed consent;
* institutional approval;
* ethics review;
* deidentification;
* data-minimization controls;
* secure storage;
* publication permission;
* withdrawal procedures;
* and additional protections for vulnerable populations.

Public repository inclusion does not remove these obligations.

23. Scope-Drift Rule

Scope drift occurs when material is added without a clear relationship to the repository’s research, governance, technical, educational, cultural, or licensing objectives.

New content must identify:

1. the research question or implementation objective it supports;
2. its domain classification;
3. its evidence class;
4. its authority level;
5. its owner;
6. its intended audience;
7. whether it is canonical or derivative;
8. and whether it creates a new dependency.

Content that cannot satisfy these conditions should remain outside the canonical repository or be stored in an explicitly noncanonical archive.
