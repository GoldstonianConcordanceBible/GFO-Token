1. Purpose

This document defines the research methodology for the Global Social Experiment and the development, testing, revision, and documentation of Web4, Web4 NIL, artificial intelligence governance, repository governance, and related institutional frameworks.

The methodology is designed for a research environment containing:

* documentary evidence;
* repository history;
* public digital content;
* interviews and transcripts;
* institutional materials;
* technical records;
* legal sources;
* LLM-generated outputs;
* human interpretation;
* governance proposals;
* implementation records;
* educational materials;
* cultural interpretation;
* and longitudinal policy changes.

The methodology must preserve a clear distinction among:

* source evidence;
* observations;
* interpretations;
* hypotheses;
* model-generated analysis;
* Delphi convergence;
* governance proposals;
* approved policies;
* technical controls;
* legal claims;
* and research findings.

⸻

2. Methodological Position

The Global Social Experiment uses a mixed-methods, design-oriented, longitudinal, and version-controlled research framework.

The research program combines elements of:

* qualitative inquiry;
* documentary analysis;
* digital ethnography;
* repository analysis;
* comparative case study;
* modified Delphi review;
* design science;
* systems thinking;
* governance analysis;
* policy analysis;
* technical conformance analysis;
* and longitudinal institutional analysis.

No single method is sufficient for the full program.

Different research questions require different evidence and methods.

The methodology must therefore remain modular.

⸻

3. Primary Methodological Question

The central methodological problem is:

How can heterogeneous human, institutional, technical, legal, cultural, and AI-generated evidence be transformed into a transparent, reproducible, and version-controlled governance architecture without allowing unsupported interpretations or model-generated claims to become canonical facts?

This methodological problem is addressed through:

1. source registration;
2. evidence classification;
3. structured coding;
4. claim provenance;
5. confidence scoring;
6. independent review;
7. legal and factual verification;
8. dissent preservation;
9. version control;
10. and publication mapping.

⸻

4. Research Design

The research design includes six connected layers.

Layer 1 — Observation

Documents what occurred, what was produced, what changed, and what participants or systems stated.

Layer 2 — Classification

Assigns each artifact a source type, evidence class, domain, privacy level, and canonical status.

Layer 3 — Interpretation

Identifies possible themes, patterns, relationships, tensions, and theoretical implications.

Layer 4 — Validation

Tests interpretations through cross-source comparison, model review, human review, legal verification, technical testing, or independent replication.

Layer 5 — Governance Translation

Converts supported findings into proposed principles, policies, schemas, workflows, controls, or readiness criteria.

Layer 6 — Longitudinal Review

Tracks how claims, policies, models, laws, technologies, and implementations change over time.

A conclusion must not move directly from observation to approved policy without passing through the applicable classification, validation, and review stages.

7. Evidence Classes

The methodology uses the following evidence classes:

E0 — Unverified Concept

An idea, claim, interpretation, or output without sufficient verification.

E1 — Researcher or Participant Interpretation

A documented interpretation that is attributable but not independently established.

E2 — Single-Source Support

A claim supported by one identifiable source.

E3 — Multiple-Source Support

A claim supported by more than one materially independent source.

E4 — Cross-Model Convergence

A claim or principle independently identified across multiple LLM reviewers.

E5 — Qualified Human Review

A claim reviewed by an appropriately qualified human professional or subject-matter reviewer.

E6 — Empirical or Technical Validation

A claim supported by observed outcomes, tests, implementation evidence, or reproducible technical results.

E7 — Independent Replication

A finding reproduced by an independent researcher, team, system, model panel, or dataset.

Evidence classes do not represent certainty in isolation.

A claim may require several evidence classes.

For example, a legal claim may require primary authority and qualified human review even when it has cross-model convergence.

10. Raw Conversation Treatment

Raw conversations may contain:

* useful source observations;
* preliminary theories;
* repeated ideas;
* unsupported factual claims;
* marketing language;
* project preferences;
* personal statements;
* theological interpretation;
* model-generated text;
* and unverified assumptions.

Raw conversations must therefore be archived as source material rather than directly treated as canonical knowledge.

Required processing sequence:

1. archive the complete conversation;
2. assign a source identifier;
3. extract propositions;
4. classify each proposition;
5. remove duplicates;
6. verify external claims;
7. map claims to research questions;
8. assign confidence and evidence status;
9. review for privacy and licensing;
10. and approve only eligible items for canonical use.

15. Observation Versus Interpretation

The methodology requires explicit separation between observation and interpretation.

Example:

Observation:
Four models independently recommended emergency suspension capability.
Interpretation:
Emergency suspension may represent a stable cross-model governance principle.
Governance proposal:
Every human-linked persona system should include an emergency suspension process.
Technical requirement:
The system must disable persona credentials and publishing permissions after an approved suspension request.
Validated control:
Tests confirm that a suspended persona cannot generate or publish new output.

These are related but not interchangeable statements.

16. Hypothesis Management

A hypothesis must be:

* clearly labeled;
* linked to one or more research questions;
* stated in falsifiable or testable form where possible;
* associated with required evidence;
* and separated from findings.

Example:

Version-controlled governance improves institutional memory and accountability compared with unversioned policy management.

This remains a hypothesis until supported through an appropriate comparative or longitudinal method.

18. Confidence Scoring

Confidence scores should support transparency but must not create false precision.

Recommended scale:

0.00–0.19 Very low
0.20–0.39 Low
0.40–0.59 Moderate
0.60–0.79 Substantial
0.80–0.94 High
0.95–1.00 Exceptional

Confidence should consider:

* source quality;
* source independence;
* consistency;
* recency;
* verification;
* legal or technical uncertainty;
* model convergence;
* human review;
* empirical support;
* and unresolved contradictions.

A high confidence score does not transform a normative proposal into a fact.

22. Modified Delphi Method

The multi-model Delphi process includes three rounds.

Round One — Independent Review

Each model receives the same approved source package and evaluation criteria without access to the other models’ responses.

Purpose:

* identify independent findings;
* reduce anchoring;
* reveal model-specific concerns;
* and establish a baseline.

Round Two — Anonymized Cross-Review

Each model receives an anonymized synthesis of Round One.

Purpose:

* test whether findings strengthen or weaken;
* identify missing conditions;
* expose false agreement;
* and refine the architecture.

Round Three — Stability Review

Each model reviews the refined architecture and remaining disagreements.

Purpose:

* identify stable convergence;
* preserve dissent;
* identify unresolved issues;
* and determine whether the architecture is ready to freeze as a research baseline.

23. Consensus Is Not Truth

Model agreement may indicate:

* shared reasoning patterns;
* common training-data influence;
* similar alignment methods;
* similar policy assumptions;
* or genuine independent convergence.

Consensus must therefore be analyzed rather than merely counted.

The study should examine:

* semantic similarity;
* reasoning differences;
* source differences;
* conditions and exceptions;
* confidence;
* and whether models changed after cross-review.

25. Factual Verification

36. Unknown-Unknown Procedure

Unknown unknowns cannot be directly enumerated in advance.

The methodology should instead create detection mechanisms.

Potential signals include:

* repeated model disagreement;
* unexpected user prompts;
* implementation incidents;
* policy-code mismatch;
* unexplained exceptions;
* new legal categories;
* model updates;
* identity misuse;
* cross-domain conflict;
* and outcomes not covered by the existing question bank.

When a possible unknown unknown appears:

1. create an issue;
2. document the event;
3. assign a preliminary domain;
4. assess safety and urgency;
5. determine whether immediate controls are required;
6. create or revise a research question;
7. identify evidence needs;
8. and preserve the event for longitudinal analysis.

37. Falsification and Revision

The Global Social Experiment and Web4 framework must remain revisable.

Claims should be weakened, revised, divided, or rejected when:

* evidence contradicts them;
* independent replication fails;
* alternative explanations are stronger;
* model convergence disappears;
* legal or technical conditions invalidate assumptions;
* implementation produces harmful outcomes;
* or definitions become too broad to distinguish Web4 from existing frameworks.

A falsified claim should remain historically traceable.

41. Publication Thresholds

A finding may be considered for academic publication when it has:

* a clear research question;
* a documented method;
* sufficient source provenance;
* transparent analysis;
* limitations;
* and a contribution that does not depend on unsupported claims.

A finding may remain in the repository as a hypothesis when publication thresholds are not met.

⸻

42. Governance Translation Thresholds

A research finding may become a proposed governance principle when:

* it addresses a defined risk or need;
* its source and reasoning are documented;
* alternatives have been considered;
* dissent is preserved;
* and the proposal can be stated operationally.

A proposed principle may become an approved policy only through the authorized governance process.

⸻

43. Technical Translation Thresholds

An approved policy may become a technical requirement when:

* the policy obligation is clear;
* the responsible system is identified;
* implementation boundaries are defined;
* testable acceptance criteria exist;
* and dependencies are documented.

A technical requirement becomes an implemented control only after evidence of deployment and testing exists.

45. Retraction Procedure

A claim should be retracted when it is:

* materially false;
* unsupported;
* unlawfully sourced;
* ethically unusable;
* fabricated;
* dangerously misleading;
* or impossible to correct without withdrawal.

Retraction must not silently delete the historical record unless legal, safety, or privacy requirements demand removal.

46. Methodological Limitations

Key limitations include:

* model outputs may change across versions;
* frontier models may share training data and assumptions;
* public sources may be incomplete;
* repository records may reflect maintainer power;
* founder-researcher overlap may influence interpretation;
* legal conditions may change;
* technical systems may change after review;
* social-media data may overrepresent highly visible participants;
* absence of evidence may be mistaken for evidence of absence;
* and cross-model convergence may reflect shared bias rather than independent truth.

These limitations must be preserved in publications and website responses where relevant.

50. Methodological Principle

The research program must remain capable of showing:

* what was observed;
* how it was classified;
* how it was interpreted;
* what challenged the interpretation;
* what was verified;
* what remained uncertain;
* how a governance proposal was created;
* how the proposal became policy;
* how the policy became code;
* and whether the code performed as intended.

The methodology exists to ensure that the Global Social Experiment becomes more than a collection of compelling ideas.

It must become a traceable system of questions, evidence, analysis, dissent, governance, testing, and revision.
