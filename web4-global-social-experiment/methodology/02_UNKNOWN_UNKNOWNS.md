Purpose

This document explains one of the central methodological ideas behind the Global Social Experiment:

The study was intentionally designed to discover unknown unknowns.

Rather than beginning with a fixed theory that the research sought to prove, the methodology was constructed to identify observations, governance challenges, methodological failures, and theoretical constructs that were not anticipated at the beginning of the project.

This approach treats uncertainty as a source of discovery rather than a flaw to eliminate.

⸻

Defining the Unknown Unknown

The phrase “unknown unknown” refers to something that researchers do not know exists at the beginning of a study.

A commonly used framework distinguishes four categories:

Knowledge State	Definition
Known knowns	Things already understood and supported by evidence.
Known unknowns	Questions researchers know they need to investigate.
Unknown knowns	Knowledge that exists but has not yet been recognized, connected, or incorporated into the current study.
Unknown unknowns	Problems, opportunities, relationships, or concepts that are not anticipated until they emerge through the research process.

The Global Social Experiment was intentionally structured to move beyond known unknowns and create conditions where unknown unknowns could emerge.

⸻

Why Traditional Research Often Misses Unknown Unknowns

Many research projects begin with:

* a fixed hypothesis;
* predetermined variables;
* predefined categories;
* narrowly scoped literature reviews;
* and static data collection.

These approaches are valuable for testing established theories but may overlook:

* emerging technologies;
* unexpected governance problems;
* interdisciplinary connections;
* institutional blind spots;
* and novel patterns that fall outside existing frameworks.

Because Web4 concerns rapidly evolving interactions among AI, governance, identity, decentralized systems, education, law, and culture, the research design required a more adaptive approach.

⸻

Unknown Unknowns in the Global Social Experiment

The repository was designed so that new concepts could emerge from observation rather than assumption.

Examples include:

* unexpected governance conflicts between AI agents and human authority;
* disagreements among frontier language models despite similar prompts;
* legal ambiguities revealed through structured review;
* previously unrecognized relationships between documentation and operational readiness;
* repository governance becoming both research infrastructure and research data;
* and the emergence of Web4 as a governance construct rather than a predefined technology category.

These examples illustrate the type of discovery the methodology seeks to capture.

Why a Repository Supports Discovery

Traditional publications freeze knowledge at one moment in time.

A version-controlled repository allows researchers to observe:

Question
↓
Observation
↓
Evidence
↓
Revision
↓
Discussion
↓
Correction
↓
New Theory

This continual refinement increases the likelihood that previously hidden issues become visible.

The Role of the Modified Delphi Process

The modified multi-model Delphi process contributes to discovering unknown unknowns because:

* reviewers begin independently;
* models use different reasoning pathways;
* disagreements are preserved rather than discarded;
* and successive rounds expose overlooked assumptions.

The objective is not merely to measure agreement.

It is to expose hidden questions that would otherwise remain invisible.

Unknown Unknowns and Web4

One methodological observation from the project is that Web4 itself was treated as an emergent construct.

Rather than defining Web4 first and collecting evidence afterward, the repository allowed recurring governance patterns to suggest that an additional conceptual layer might be needed beyond Web1, Web2, and Web3.

Whether this construct ultimately gains broader scholarly acceptance remains an open research question.

Unknown Unknowns and LLM Review

Large language models can reveal:

* overlooked implementation issues;
* competing governance approaches;
* inconsistencies in repository structure;
* alternative terminology;
* and hidden dependencies.

At the same time, they may introduce:

* hallucinations;
* fabricated authority;
* legal inaccuracies;
* excessive confidence;
* and convergence driven by similar training data.

Both discovery and error are treated as research observations.

⸻

The Proposed-Law Example

One of the most important methodological observations arose when a reviewer presented proposed federal legislation as though it were enacted law.

The significance of this event extends beyond a single factual mistake.

It demonstrated that:

* sophisticated models can produce confident legal errors;
* consensus does not guarantee correctness;
* verification must remain independent of reviewer agreement;
* and legal claims require their own validation pathway.

This unexpected observation became a methodological finding rather than simply an error to erase.

Discovery Principles

The methodology follows several principles:

1. Preserve unexpected findings.
2. Separate observation from interpretation.
3. Record disagreement.
4. Document corrections.
5. Update theory when justified.
6. Avoid forcing evidence into predetermined categories.
7. Maintain transparent provenance.

Practical Guidance for Future Researchers

When new observations emerge:

1. Do not immediately classify them as errors.
2. Determine whether they represent:
    * new evidence;
    * methodological limitations;
    * theoretical refinement;
    * implementation lessons;
    * or future research.
3. Document the reasoning.
4. Preserve earlier interpretations.
5. Update the repository transparently.

This approach allows the research program to grow without rewriting history.
