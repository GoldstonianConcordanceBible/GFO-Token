Purpose

This document explains how the Global Social Experiment evaluates agreement, preserves disagreement, and incorporates both into repository governance.

Unlike many review processes that emphasize consensus alone, this methodology treats consensus and dissent as complementary research outputs.

Agreement identifies stable observations.

Disagreement identifies opportunities for future investigation.

Both are essential.

⸻

Why Consensus Alone Is Insufficient

Consensus can indicate:

* shared reasoning;
* recurring governance principles;
* methodological stability;
* or convergent interpretation.

However, consensus cannot establish:

* factual truth;
* legal correctness;
* technical security;
* or institutional validity.

Similarly, disagreement does not automatically indicate error.

Competing interpretations may both be reasonable.

⸻

Independent Review

Consensus begins with reviewer independence.

Reviewers evaluate repository materials before viewing other reviews.

Repository Draft
↓
Reviewer A
Reviewer B
Reviewer C
Reviewer D
↓
Independent Assessments

Independent review reduces anchoring effects.

⸻

Controlled Reflection

Beginning in later Delphi rounds:

reviewers receive:

* anonymized summaries;
* recurring themes;
* methodological observations;
* and unresolved disagreements.

Reviewers may revise earlier conclusions but are not required to do so.

⸻

Consensus Levels

The repository uses six standardized levels.

Full Consensus

All reviewers independently reach materially similar conclusions.

⸻

Strong Consensus

Most reviewers agree with only minor differences.

⸻

Substantial Consensus

Agreement exists on major principles, while implementation details remain unresolved.

⸻

Partial Consensus

Agreement applies only to portions of the proposal.

⸻

Weak Consensus

Reviewers identify similar issues but recommend different solutions.

⸻

No Consensus

No stable convergence emerges.

The disagreement itself becomes a documented finding.

Relationship to Evidence

Consensus never replaces evidence.

Instead:

Evidence
↓
Claim
↓
Consensus

Evidence always precedes agreement.

⸻

Relationship to Legal Verification

Legal correctness is evaluated separately.

Reviewer Agreement
↓
Legal Verification
↓
Repository Policy

Even unanimous reviewer agreement cannot substitute for legal verification.

⸻

Repository Decisions

Editors evaluate:

* evidence;
* reviewer recommendations;
* consensus;
* dissent;
* legal verification;
* and implementation implications.

Possible outcomes include:

Accept
Accept with Revision
Defer
Needs More Evidence
Reject
Retire

⸻

Preserving Dissent

The repository should never erase disagreement simply because:

* a publication requires simplicity;
* consensus increased later;
* commercial messaging prefers certainty;
* or implementation proceeded in one direction.

Historical disagreement remains valuable evidence.

⸻

Future Research

Many dissent records become future research questions.

Typical sequence:

Disagreement
↓
Replication
↓
Additional Evidence
↓
Possible Future Consensus

Not every disagreement should be resolved immediately.

⸻

Relationship to Publications

Publications should distinguish:

* established findings;
* provisional conclusions;
* unresolved disagreements;
* and future research priorities.

Readers should be able to determine which conclusions remain contested.

⸻
