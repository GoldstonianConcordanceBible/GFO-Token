1. Purpose

This document defines the formal scope of the Global Social Experiment and establishes boundaries between:

* research;
* governance;
* technical implementation;
* token operations;
* publishing;
* education;
* consulting;
* cultural interpretation;
* theology;
* mythology;
* artistic expression;
* marketing;
* and private or restricted activity.

The purpose of these boundaries is to prevent scope drift, unsupported claims, accidental misclassification, and the presentation of project activity as formal research without adequate evidence or review.

This document answers four foundational questions:

1. What belongs inside the formal Global Social Experiment?
2. What may be studied but should remain outside the canonical research layer?
3. What content may support the project without becoming research evidence?
4. What material must remain private, restricted, licensed, or excluded?

⸻

2. Scope Principle

The Global Social Experiment is a connected research program rather than a simple collection of:

* tokens;
* books;
* social-media campaigns;
* artificial intelligence outputs;
* DAOs;
* websites;
* consulting engagements;
* educational programs;
* theological narratives;
* cultural projects;
* or business initiatives.

A project, post, transaction, publication, conversation, or digital artifact does not become formal research merely because it is associated with the GFO ecosystem.

To enter the formal research program, an activity or artifact must have:

1. a defined research purpose;
2. a relevant research question;
3. an identified unit of analysis;
4. a documented source;
5. a classification;
6. an evidence status;
7. a privacy status;
8. an owner or steward;
9. a review pathway;
10. and a preservation or retention rule.

They may:

* explain;

* synthesize;
* contextualize;

* reviewed;
* versioned;

17. Research Versus Implementation Boundary

A research finding describes what the study observed or concluded.

An implementation requirement describes what a system should do.

A technical control demonstrates how a requirement is enforced.

These must remain separately documented.

Example:

Research finding:
Models converged on the need for emergency persona suspension.
Governance requirement:
Authorized persons must be able to request emergency suspension.
Technical control:
The persona service includes a revocation endpoint and credential-disable workflow.
Evidence:
A test confirms that suspended credentials cannot generate or publish outputs.

A written policy without a technical control must not be described as technically enforced.

18. Documentation Readiness Boundary

Documentation readiness means required policies and records exist in reviewed form.

It does not establish that:

* contracts are executed;
* controls are deployed;
* code is secure;
* laws have been satisfied;
* privacy requirements are met;
* professional review is complete;
* or the system is safe for launch.

The website must not equate documentation readiness with activation readiness.

22. Research Question Admission Test

A proposed question may enter the formal research agenda when it:

1. relates to the primary research question or a recognized domain;
2. identifies a meaningful gap;
3. can be stated neutrally;
4. does not assume its conclusion;
5. can be investigated through an identifiable method;
6. identifies relevant evidence or future data needs;
7. can be classified by domain;
8. and does not require prohibited or unethical data collection.

New questions should receive a unique identifier.

Existing question numbers must not be reused.

⸻

23. Evidence Admission Test

Evidence may enter the formal repository when it has:

* an identifiable source;
* a date;
* an owner or steward;
* a content classification;
* a privacy classification;
* a license or permitted-use basis;
* a verification status;
* and a relationship to one or more claims or research questions.

Evidence should be rejected or restricted when it is:

* unlawfully obtained;
* private without authorization;
* materially altered without disclosure;
* impossible to trace;
* outside licensed use;
* unsafe to publish;
* or irrelevant to the research program.

27. Scope Change Procedure

A major scope change requires:

1. a written proposal;
2. identification of affected research questions;
3. rationale;
4. new risks;
5. privacy and legal review where applicable;
6. repository dependency analysis;
7. impact on publications and website retrieval;
8. approval under the repository governance process;
9. an update to this file;
10. and an update to 01_CANONICAL_INDEX.md.

Examples of major scope changes include:

* adding a new regulated industry;
* introducing live participant experimentation;
* collecting biometric data;
* issuing conformance marks;
* adding financial recommendations;
* adding clinical decision support;
* or allowing autonomous financial transactions under a human persona.

28. Scope-Drift Indicators

Potential scope drift includes:

* adding unrelated projects without research justification;
* treating promotional activity as evidence;
* creating research questions solely to support predetermined claims;
* presenting theology as technical proof;
* presenting model agreement as legal authority;
* adding private data to increase apparent credibility;
* duplicating operational token documentation without need;
* creating new licensing tiers without governance approval;
* and allowing frontend outputs to become canonical without review.

Scope drift should be logged as an issue and resolved before release.
